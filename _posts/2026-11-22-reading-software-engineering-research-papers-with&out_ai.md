Reading research papers is challenging, especially in Software Engineering, where papers can introduce complex architectures, algorithmes, frameworks, methodologies, experiments, and technologies.

Research papers are often written in a condensed style because of publication limitation and their intended audience, which is assumed to already have some knowledge of the research area.

Moreover, the reason you are reading a paper might be different from the reason the authors wrote it. You may be interested in understanding a particular algorithm, discovering a research gap, exploring a methodology, or identifying a possible direction for your master’s thesis or PhD.

You may also be reading for a different reason: perhaps you are a software developer trying to understand a technique, an engineer comparing approaches, a student learning a new subject, or simply someone curious about how a technology works. You do not need to be an academic researcher to benefit from reading research papers. The questions you ask will depend on what you want to learn or accomplish.

Finally, your time is limited. You cannot always afford to read every paper word by word or revisit every section several times.

For these reasons, developing an effective research-paper reading strategy is important.

Today, AI tools can help automate parts of this process. They can extract information, summarize sections, explain unfamiliar terminology and organize research findings.

However, AI should be an assistant, not a replacement for your own reading and critical thinking.

This article explores two complementary approaches:

1. Using AI to assist with reading and extracting information from research papers.
2. Developing your own ability to read, understand, and critically evaluate research papers.

# Part 1: Using AI to Assist with Reading Research Papers

> If you're going to always use AI, you're removing the artwork of reading a scientific paper.
{: .prompt-warning }

## Why Use AI?

AI can help reduce the time spent on repetitive tasks when reading research papers.

AI can also help you:

- Understand unfamiliar technical terminology.
- Explain complex algorithms and architectures.
- Identify the research problem and proposed solution.
- Extract experimental results and evaluation methods.
- Identify limitations and future research directions.
- Organize information from multiple research papers.
- Generate a structured summary for later revision.

However, remember that AI-generated answers can be incomplete, misleading, or incorrect.

Always verify important information against the original paper, especially when dealing with experimental results, mathematical explanations, technical claims, and research contributions.

## When Should You Use AI?

You do not need AI for every paper.

Consider using it when:

- The paper contains unfamiliar concepts or technical terminology.
- You are exploring a research area outside your current knowledge.
- You need to extract structured information from several papers.
- You are reading an older paper and want to understand the technology it proposed in its original context.
- You need help navigating a paper with a complicated structure.
- You want to compare research approaches or organize your literature review.

### An Important Disclaimer

Not every technology proposed in an older research paper is still relevant or used in your research field today.

For example, an older Software Engineering paper might introduce a framework, architecture, programming language feature, or development methodology that has since evolved or been replaced.

In such situations, AI can help you understand what the authors proposed, why it was interesting at that time, and how the technology worked.

But do not automatically assume that the proposed technology is still relevant today.

You should distinguish between:

- Understanding the historical contribution of a paper.
- Understanding the technical ideas behind its proposed solution.
- Evaluating whether those ideas are still relevant to your research objectives.

Your academic journey should determine what you read, what you investigate, and what you take from each paper.

## Automating Research Paper Extraction with AI

A useful approach is to ask an AI assistant to extract the same information you would normally search for when reading a paper.

Rather than asking for a generic summary, give the AI a structured set of questions.

The following questions will serve as the extraction framework throughout this article.

### A. Motivation and Research Problem

Ask the AI to identify:

- What is the proposed solution, hypothesis, or main idea?
- Why do the authors believe this solution will work?
- Why might it be better than previous approaches?
- How is the solution designed and implemented?
- What technologies, algorithms, architectures, or methodologies are involved?

### B. Proposed Solution

Ask the AI to identify:

- How do the authors evaluate their proposed solution?
- What experiments or arguments support their claims?
- What datasets, benchmarks, tools, or evaluation criteria are used?
- What results are obtained?
- What benefits and problems are identified?

### C. Evaluation of the Proposed Solution

Ask the AI to identify:

- How do the authors evaluate their proposed solution?
- What experiments or arguments support their claims?
- What datasets, benchmarks, tools, or evaluation criteria are used?
- What results are obtained?
- What benefits and problems are identified?

### D. Critical Analysis

Ask the AI to help investigate:

- What are the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach?
- What assumptions does the research make?
- What limitations are explicitly identified?
- Are there potential problems that the authors did not discuss?
- What aspects of the proposed solution are interesting or controversial?
- Does the evaluation adequately support the authors' claims?

Remember that AI-generated criticism is only a starting point. Your own assessment is necessary.

### E. Research Contributions

Ask the AI to identify:

- What are the main contributions of the paper?
- Does it introduce a new algorithm, architecture, methodology, framework, software, or experimental technique?
- Does it provide new insights into an existing research problem?
- Does it present a survey or organize existing knowledge?

### F. Future Research Directions

Ask the AI to identify:

- What future research directions do the authors propose?
- What limitations could lead to further research?
- What possible improvements can be derived from the proposed approach?
- What research opportunities can you identify independently?

### G. Remaining Questions

Ask the AI to identify:

- Which concepts are difficult to understand?
- What assumptions require further investigation?
- What information is missing?
- What questions should be discussed with other researchers?
- What additional papers or references could help clarify the subject?

### H. Takeaway Message

Finally, ask the AI to provide:

- The main message of the paper.
- Its most important contribution.
- Its relevance to the research area.
- A concise summary that can help you remember the paper later.

## Research AI Tools You Can Explore

A general-purpose AI assistant is not the only option. Some tools focus on different parts of the research-reading process.

The following are examples, not endorsements or requirements:

| Tool | Potential use |
|---|---|
| Elicit | Searching scholarly literature and extracting or comparing information across papers. |
| SciSpace | Interacting with research papers and asking questions about PDF content. |
| ResearchRabbit | Discovering related papers and exploring connections between papers and authors. |

These tools do different jobs. For example, a literature-discovery tool can help you find related papers, while a paper-chat tool can help you ask questions about a document you already have.

You can also use a general-purpose AI assistant that supports PDF or document analysis. The important point is not which tool you choose, but whether it helps with the task you actually have.

Features, access limits, and pricing can change. Check each tool's current website before relying on a particular feature.

> **Important:** Do not assume that an AI research tool has read the complete paper. Some systems may rely on abstracts, indexed text, or only the content they can access. Check the source material and the tool's evidence links before trusting an answer.

# Part 2: Reading and Understanding Research Papers Yourself

AI can accelerate information extraction, but understanding a research paper requires more than collecting answers.

You need to develop the ability to identify research problems, understand proposed solutions, evaluate evidence, and form your own opinions.

## 1. Understand the Structure of a Research Paper

Despite their condensed format, research papers are often repetitive.

The introduction usually explains the motivation behind the research and provides an overview of the proposed solution.

The body presents the proposed solution in detail and explains its evaluation through arguments, implementations, or experiments.

The conclusion summarizes the work, highlights its primary contributions, and often discusses future research directions.

Related work discusses previous approaches and positions the current research in relation to existing literature.

Because information is repeated at different levels of detail, you do not necessarily need to read the paper from beginning to end on your first pass.

Reading a paper out of order can sometimes be more productive.

A useful starting sequence is:

1. Read the title and abstract.
2. Read the introduction to understand the motivation and research problem.
3. Examine the conclusion to identify the main contributions and future directions.
4. Review the proposed solution and methodology.
5. Study the evaluation and experimental results.
6. Return to related work to understand how the research compares with previous approaches.

This is an initial exploration, not a substitute for reading the important technical details.

## 2. The Questions You Should Answer While Reading

Adapting William G. Griswold's strategy is always a good choice.

1. **What are the motivations for this work?** For a research paper, there is an expectation that a problem has been solved that no one else has published in the literature. This problem intrinsically has two parts. The first is often unstated, what I call the people problem. The people problem is the benefits that are desired in the world at large; for example some issue of quality of life, such as saved time or increased safety. The second part is the technical problem, which is: why doesn't the people problem have a trivial solution? There is also an implication that previous solutions to the problem are inadequate. What are the previous solutions and why are they inadequate? Finally, the motivation and statement of the problem are distilled into a research question, the question that the paper sets out to answer. This might be more focused than the problem stated at the outset. Oftentimes, one or more of these elements are not explicitly stated, making your job more difficult.
2. **What is the proposed solution?** This is also called the hypothesis or idea. This is the proposed answer to the research question. There should also be an answer to the question why is it believed that this solution will work, and be better than previous solutions? There should also be a discussion about how the solution is achieved (designed and implemented) or is at least achievable.
3. **What is the work's evaluation of the proposed solution?** An idea alone is usually not adequate for publication of a research paper. This is the concrete engagement of the research question. What argument, implementation, and/or experiment makes the case for the value of the ideas? What benefits or problems are identified?
4. **What is your analysis of the identified problem, idea and evaluation?** Is this a good idea? What flaws do you perceive in the work? What are the most interesting points made? What are the most controversial ideas or points made? For work that has practical implications, you also want to ask: Is this really going to work, who would want it, what it will take to give it to them, and when might it become a reality?
5. **What are the contributions?** The contributions in a paper may be many and varied. Beyond the insights on the research question, a few additional possibilities include: ideas, software, experimental techniques, or an area survey.
6. **What are the contributions?** The contributions in a paper may be many and varied. Beyond the insights on the research question, a few additional possibilities include: ideas, software, experimental techniques, or an area survey.
7. **What questions are you left with?** What questions would you like to raise in an open discussion of the work? What do you find confusing or difficult to understand? By taking the time to list several, you will be forced to think more deeply about the work.
8. **What is your take-away message from this paper?** Sum up the main implication of the paper from your perspective. This is useful for very quick review and refreshing your memory. It also forces you to try to identify the essence of the work.

## 3. Where Can You Find These Answers?

You do not need to search randomly through the entire paper.

Different sections usually contain different types of information.

| Research information | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Motivation and research problem | Introduction |
| Research question | Introduction |
| Proposed solution | Introduction and methodology |
| Technical details | Main body and methodology |
| Implementation | Design and implementation sections |
| Evaluation | Experiments and evaluation sections |
| Results | Results and discussion |
| Limitations | Discussion and conclusion |
| Contributions | Introduction and conclusion |
| Future directions | Conclusion and future work |
| Previous approaches | Related work |

## 4. Take Notes and Annotate Your Papers

Reading a paper is not simply about collecting information. You should build your own understanding of it.

I recommend keeping a notebook or digital document for the papers you read.

You can also annotate the original PDF directly.

Here is a practical annotation strategy:

- Highlight important statements as you read.
- Mark paragraphs related to the motivation and research problem.
- Identify the proposed idea and solution.
- Highlight the evaluation methodology and important results.
- Mark the contributions.
- Write the takeaway message on the first page of your notes.
- Write down your questions and observations.
- Record your own criticisms and potential improvements.

Try to answer the questions yourself before relying on external explanations.

Use documentation, other research papers, and technical resources when necessary.

You may also need a second pass through the paper before all the ideas become clear.

You are not finished reading a paper simply because you have reached its final page. You should be able to explain its problem, solution, evaluation, and contributions in your own words.

## 5. Understand the Context of the Paper

A research paper should not be considered independently of the other papers in its research area.

It might:

- Generalize an earlier approach.
- Introduce a new research direction.
- Contradict previous findings.
- Improve an existing methodology.
- Address limitations identified by previous researchers.

Understanding this context is particularly important when conducting a literature review.

Try to identify how the paper relates to the research you have already read.

This helps you understand the evolution of ideas rather than treating every paper as an isolated contribution.

## 6. Keep Asking Questions

Taking time to write down questions often reveals thoughts that were not initially articulated.

Perhaps the paper is unclear about an important assumption, does not adequately explain a technical decision, or ignores a limitation that you consider important.

These observations are part of developing your own research perspective.

Do not treat unanswered questions as a failure to understand the paper. They can become starting points for further investigation.

# Final Thoughts

Reading research papers is a skill that develops with practice.

You will not necessarily need every question, every AI feature, or every section of this methodology for every paper you encounter.

Some papers will be important because of their theoretical contributions. Others will be valuable because of their implementation, experimental methodology, or relationship with your research direction.

Use AI when it genuinely helps you, particularly when dealing with unfamiliar concepts, large amounts of literature, or older technologies.

But keep your academic objectives in mind.

Read with a purpose, question the authors' assumptions, verify their claims, and develop your own understanding.

The objective is not simply to read more research papers. It is to become better at understanding research, identifying valuable contributions, and developing ideas of your own.
