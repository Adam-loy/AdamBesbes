# How to Read a Software Engineering Research Paper with AI Assistance

Reading research papers can be challenging, especially in Software Engineering, where papers may introduce complex architectures, algorithms, frameworks, methodologies, experiments, and technologies.

Research papers are often written in a condensed style because of publication limitations and their intended audience, which is assumed to already have some knowledge of the research area.

Moreover, the reason you are reading a paper might be different from the reason the authors wrote it. You may be interested in understanding a particular algorithm, discovering a research gap, exploring a methodology, or identifying a possible direction for your master's thesis or PhD.

You may also be reading for a different reason: perhaps you are a software developer trying to understand a technique, an engineer comparing approaches, a student learning a new subject, or simply someone curious about how a technology works. You do not need to be an academic researcher to benefit from reading research papers. The questions you ask will depend on what you want to learn or accomplish.

Finally, your time is limited. You cannot always afford to read every paper word by word or revisit every section several times.

For these reasons, developing an effective research-paper reading strategy is important.

Today, AI tools can help automate parts of this process. They can extract information, summarize sections, explain unfamiliar terminology, and organize research findings. Some tools are designed specifically for scholarly search, literature discovery, or interacting with research PDFs.

However, **AI should be an assistant, not a replacement for your own reading and critical thinking.**

This article explores two complementary approaches:

1. Using AI to assist with reading and extracting information from research papers.
2. Developing your own ability to read, understand, and critically evaluate research papers.

> If you always let AI do the reading for you, you risk losing the practice of reading, questioning, and discovering ideas yourself. Use it to support the process—not to take the process away from you. {: .prompt-warning }

# Part 1: Using AI to Assist with Reading Research Papers

## 1. Why Use AI?

AI can help reduce the time spent on repetitive tasks when reading research papers.

For example, instead of manually searching through a long paper to identify its research question, proposed solution, evaluation, and contributions, you can ask an AI assistant to extract these elements into a structured format.

AI can also help you:

- Understand unfamiliar technical terminology.
- Explain complex algorithms and architectures.
- Identify the research problem and proposed solution.
- Extract experimental results and evaluation methods.
- Identify limitations and future research directions.
- Organize information from multiple research papers.
- Generate a structured summary for later revision.

However, AI-generated answers can be incomplete, misleading, or incorrect. A summary can omit an important qualification, confuse the authors' claims with experimental evidence, or make an inference sound like a fact.

Always verify important information against the original paper, especially when dealing with experimental results, mathematical explanations, technical claims, and research contributions.

## 2. When Should You Use AI?

You do not need AI for every paper.

Consider using it when:

- The paper contains unfamiliar concepts or technical terminology.
- You are exploring a research area outside your current knowledge.
- You need to extract structured information from several papers.
- You are reading an older paper and want to understand the technology it proposed in its original context.
- You need help navigating a paper with a complicated structure.
- You want to compare research approaches or organize your literature review.
- You need help understanding a particular paragraph, figure, equation, or experimental setup.

You can also use AI without asking it to summarize the entire paper. Sometimes a focused question about one unfamiliar concept is all you need.

### An important disclaimer

Not every technology proposed in an older research paper is still relevant or used in your field today.

For example, an older Software Engineering paper might introduce a framework, architecture, programming language feature, or development methodology that has since evolved or been replaced.

In such situations, AI can help you understand what the authors proposed, why it was interesting at that time, and how the technology worked.

But do not automatically assume that the proposed technology is still relevant today. Understanding a historical contribution and deciding whether to adopt an idea in a current project are two different tasks.

You should distinguish between:

- Understanding the historical contribution of a paper.
- Understanding the technical ideas behind its proposed solution.
- Evaluating whether those ideas are still relevant to your current work or research objectives.

**Your goals should determine what you read, what you investigate, and what you take from each paper.**

This applies whether you are following an academic path, building software professionally, studying independently, or exploring a topic out of curiosity. Do not follow this article or any AI-generated methodology blindly. Use the parts that are useful for your objectives, apply them when necessary, and develop your own approach.

> **Tip:** Before opening an AI tool, decide what you want from the paper. Are you trying to understand one concept, evaluate a proposed technique, find related work, or learn the history of a technology? A clear objective makes it easier to decide whether AI is useful and what to ask it. {: .prompt-tip }

## 3. Research AI Tools You Can Explore

A general-purpose AI assistant is not the only option. Some tools focus on different parts of the research-reading process.

The following are examples, not endorsements or requirements:

| Tool | Potential use |
|---|---|
| [Elicit](https://elicit.com/) | Searching scholarly literature and extracting or comparing information across papers. |
| [SciSpace](https://scispace.com/) | Interacting with research papers and asking questions about PDF content. |
| [ResearchRabbit](https://www.researchrabbit.ai/) | Discovering related papers and exploring connections between papers and authors. |

These tools do different jobs. For example, a literature-discovery tool can help you find related papers, while a paper-chat tool can help you ask questions about a document you already have.

You can also use a general-purpose AI assistant that supports PDF or document analysis. The important point is not which tool you choose, but whether it helps with the task you actually have.

Features, access limits, and pricing can change. Check each tool's current website before relying on a particular feature.

> **Important:** Do not assume that an AI research tool has read the complete paper. Some systems may rely on abstracts, indexed text, or only the content they can access. Check the source material and the tool's evidence links before trusting an answer. {: .prompt-info }

## 4. Automating Research Paper Extraction with AI

A useful approach is to ask an AI assistant to extract the same information you would normally search for when reading a paper.

Rather than asking for a generic summary, give the AI a structured set of questions.

The following questions can serve as an extraction framework.

### A. Motivation and Research Problem

Ask the AI to identify:

- What motivates this research?
- What real-world or human problem are the authors trying to address?
- What is the technical problem?
- Why is the problem difficult to solve?
- What previous solutions exist?
- Why are those solutions inadequate?
- What research question does the paper attempt to answer?

### B. Proposed Solution

Ask the AI to identify:

- What is the proposed solution, hypothesis, or main idea?
- Why do the authors believe this solution will work?
- Why might it be better than previous approaches?
- How is the solution designed and implemented?
- What technologies, algorithms, architectures, or methodologies are involved?

### C. Evaluation of the Proposed Solution

Ask the AI to identify:

- How do the authors evaluate their proposed solution?
- What experiments or arguments support their claims?
- What datasets, benchmarks, tools, or evaluation criteria are used?
- What results are obtained?
- What benefits and problems are identified?

### D. Critical Analysis

Ask the AI to help investigate:

- What are the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach?
- What assumptions does the research make?
- What limitations are explicitly identified?
- Are there potential problems that the authors did not discuss?
- What aspects of the proposed solution are interesting or controversial?
- Does the evaluation adequately support the authors' claims?

Remember that AI-generated criticism is only a starting point. Your own assessment is necessary. Ask the AI to separate limitations stated by the authors from concerns it has inferred.

### E. Research Contributions

Ask the AI to identify:

- What are the main contributions of the paper?
- Does it introduce a new algorithm, architecture, methodology, framework, software, or experimental technique?
- Does it provide new insights into an existing research problem?
- Does it present a survey or organize existing knowledge?

### F. Future Research Directions

Ask the AI to identify:

- What future research directions do the authors propose?
- What limitations could lead to further research?
- What possible improvements can be derived from the proposed approach?
- What research opportunities can you identify independently?

### G. Remaining Questions

Ask the AI to identify:

- Which concepts are difficult to understand?
- What assumptions require further investigation?
- What information is missing?
- What questions should be discussed with other researchers or practitioners?
- What additional papers or references could help clarify the subject?

### H. Takeaway Message

Finally, ask the AI to provide:

- The main message of the paper.
- Its most important contribution.
- Its relevance to the research area or practical problem.
- A concise summary that can help you remember the paper later.

## 5. A Practical AI-Assisted Workflow

You can use the previous questions to automate the initial extraction process.

A practical workflow is:

1. Provide the research paper in PDF format to an AI assistant or paper-analysis tool that supports document analysis.
2. Ask it to extract the information using the structured questions above.
3. Request references to relevant sections, page numbers, figures, or tables whenever possible.
4. Ask it to distinguish information explicitly stated by the authors from its own interpretation.
5. Review the extracted information against the original paper.
6. Investigate unfamiliar concepts and verify important technical details.
7. Save the resulting notes for future research or project work.

Do not ask the AI to invent missing information. If the paper does not explicitly answer a question, the AI should indicate that the information was not found.

This is particularly important when extracting experimental results, limitations, and research contributions.

> **Warning:** Never treat an AI-generated extraction as a verified literature review. Check the original paper, confirm quotations and page references, and make sure the cited work actually supports the statement. {: .prompt-danger }

# Part 2: Reading and Understanding Research Papers Yourself

AI can accelerate information extraction, but understanding a research paper requires more than collecting answers.

You need to develop the ability to identify problems, understand proposed solutions, evaluate evidence, and form your own opinions. These skills are useful to academic researchers, but they are also valuable to developers, engineers, students, and independent learners who want to assess technical claims rather than simply accept them.

## 1. Understand the Structure of a Research Paper

Despite their condensed format, research papers are often repetitive.

The introduction usually explains the motivation behind the research and provides an overview of the proposed solution.

The body presents the proposed solution in detail and explains its evaluation through arguments, implementations, or experiments.

The conclusion summarizes the work, highlights its primary contributions, and often discusses future research directions.

Related work discusses previous approaches and positions the current research in relation to existing literature.

Because information is repeated at different levels of detail, you do not necessarily need to read the paper from beginning to end on your first pass.

Reading a paper out of order can sometimes be more productive.

A useful starting sequence is:

1. Read the title and abstract.
2. Read the introduction to understand the motivation and research problem.
3. Examine the conclusion to identify the main contributions and future directions.
4. Review the proposed solution and methodology.
5. Study the evaluation and experimental results.
6. Return to related work to understand how the research compares with previous approaches.

This is an initial exploration, not a substitute for reading the important technical details.

## 2. The Questions You Should Answer While Reading

The following approach adapts the paper-reading strategy described by William G. Griswold in *How to Read an Engineering Research Paper*. The questions are a framework, not a rigid checklist: some may be more relevant than others depending on the paper and your purpose.

### A. What Are the Motivations for This Work?

A research paper generally attempts to solve a problem that has not been adequately addressed in existing literature.

This problem has two important dimensions.

**The people problem:**

What benefits are expected from solving the problem?

For example:

- Improving software quality.
- Reducing software development time.
- Increasing system reliability.
- Improving maintainability.
- Reducing development costs.
- Improving security or performance.

**The technical problem:**

Why is the people problem difficult to solve?

You should investigate:

- What makes the problem technically challenging?
- What previous solutions exist?
- Why are previous solutions insufficient?
- What limitations do existing approaches have?
- What research question does the paper attempt to answer?

Sometimes these elements are not explicitly stated. You may need to identify the connections yourself.

For a non-academic reader, you can adapt these questions: what problem is the technology trying to solve, who experiences that problem, and why is the existing solution not enough?

### B. What Is the Proposed Solution?

This is the central idea or hypothesis of the research.

You should understand:

- What exactly are the authors proposing?
- Why do they believe their solution will work?
- Why might it be better than previous solutions?
- How is the solution designed?
- How is it implemented or expected to be implemented?

In Software Engineering research, the proposed solution might be:

- A new software architecture.
- A development methodology.
- A testing strategy.
- A machine learning approach.
- A software framework.
- A new algorithm.
- A tool or automated process.

Do not stop at identifying the technology's name. Try to understand the underlying idea and how it addresses the original research problem.

For a practitioner or independent learner, ask a related question: what would I need to understand before I could evaluate, reproduce, or use this approach?

### C. What Is the Work's Evaluation of the Proposed Solution?

An idea alone is usually not enough to establish the value of research.

You need to understand how the authors demonstrate that their solution addresses the research question.

Look for:

- The experimental design.
- The implementation details.
- The datasets and benchmarks.
- The evaluation metrics.
- The comparison with existing approaches.
- The reported results.
- The identified benefits and problems.

Ask yourself whether the evaluation provides sufficient evidence for the authors' claims.

For example, if a paper proposes a new software testing technique, does its evaluation demonstrate improvements over existing techniques? Under what conditions? Are there limitations in the experimental setup?

If you are reading as a developer, you might also ask whether the evaluation resembles the conditions in which you would use the technique.

### D. What Is Your Analysis of the Problem, Idea, and Evaluation?

This is where your own critical thinking becomes particularly important.

Ask yourself:

- Is this a good idea?
- What weaknesses do you perceive?
- Are the assumptions reasonable?
- Does the evaluation adequately support the claims?
- What are the most interesting points?
- What are the most controversial ideas?
- Are there alternative approaches?
- Could the solution work in a real-world environment?
- Who would benefit from using it?
- What would be required to adopt it?

For research with practical implications, consider whether the proposed approach is feasible outside the experimental environment.

Do not automatically agree with the authors simply because their work has been published.

### E. What Are the Contributions?

Research contributions can take many forms.

Beyond answering the original research question, a paper might contribute:

- New theoretical insights.
- New algorithms or techniques.
- Software implementations.
- Experimental methodologies.
- Frameworks or tools.
- New datasets.
- Empirical findings.
- A survey or organization of an existing research area.

Identify what is genuinely new in the paper.

A useful question is:

**What can another researcher, developer, or reader learn, reuse, improve, or build upon because this paper exists?**

### F. What Are the Future Directions for This Research?

Look at the future work section, but do not limit yourself to the authors' suggestions.

Ask:

- What future directions do the authors identify?
- What limitations could be addressed in subsequent research?
- What improvements could be made?
- What related problems remain unsolved?
- What ideas did you develop while reading the paper?

Sometimes the most interesting research opportunities are found in the limitations of the current work.

For a non-academic reader, future directions can also mean: what might happen next with this technology, and what would need to improve before it becomes useful in a real project?

### G. What Questions Are You Left With?

Take the time to write down questions that arise while reading.

For example:

- Why did the authors choose this particular algorithm?
- Could another architecture produce better results?
- Is the proposed approach scalable?
- Does the solution depend on a particular technology?
- Would the results remain valid with different datasets?
- What happens if one of the authors' assumptions is not satisfied?

Writing down these questions forces you to think more deeply about the work.

Some questions may be answered by reading other papers, consulting technical documentation, or discussing the work with researchers or experienced practitioners.

### H. What Is Your Takeaway Message from This Paper?

Finally, summarize the main implication of the paper from your own perspective.

Try to explain:

- What problem does the paper address?
- What is the central idea?
- What is its most important contribution?
- What did you learn from it?
- How does it relate to your research, studies, or practical interests?

This summary is useful when revisiting the paper later, particularly when you are reading many papers for a literature review or comparing technical approaches for a project.

## 3. Where Can You Find These Answers?

You do not need to search randomly through the entire paper.

Different sections usually contain different types of information.

| Research information | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Motivation and research problem | Introduction |
| Research question | Introduction |
| Proposed solution | Introduction and methodology |
| Technical details | Main body and methodology |
| Implementation | Design and implementation sections |
| Evaluation | Experiments and evaluation sections |
| Results | Results and discussion |
| Limitations | Discussion and conclusion |
| Contributions | Introduction and conclusion |
| Future directions | Conclusion and future work |
| Previous approaches | Related work |

These are general indications. The exact organization depends on the paper and its research area.

## 4. Take Notes and Annotate Your Papers

Reading a paper is not simply about collecting information. You should build your own understanding of it.

Keep a notebook or digital document for the papers you read. You can also annotate the original PDF directly.

Here is a practical annotation strategy:

- Highlight important statements as you read.
- Mark paragraphs related to the motivation and research problem.
- Identify the proposed idea and solution.
- Highlight the evaluation methodology and important results.
- Mark the contributions.
- Write the takeaway message on the first page of your notes.
- Write down your questions and observations.
- Record your own criticisms and potential improvements.

Try to answer the questions yourself before relying on external explanations.

Use documentation, other research papers, and technical resources when necessary.

You may also need a second pass through the paper before all the ideas become clear.

**You are not finished reading a paper simply because you have reached its final page. You should be able to explain its problem, solution, evaluation, and contributions in your own words.**

## 5. Understand the Context of the Paper

A research paper should not be considered independently of the other papers in its research area.

It might:

- Generalize an earlier approach.
- Introduce a new research direction.
- Contradict previous findings.
- Improve an existing methodology.
- Address limitations identified by previous researchers.

Understanding this context is particularly important when conducting a literature review.

Try to identify how the paper relates to the research you have already read. This helps you understand the evolution of ideas rather than treating every paper as an isolated contribution.

For a practitioner, the same principle applies to technical documentation and engineering articles: compare the proposal with existing approaches, and check whether later work has changed the picture.

## 6. An Alternative: Write Your Own 250-Word Abstract

If answering all the previous questions individually does not work for you, there is another approach.

Write an approximately 250-word abstract of the paper.

However, do not simply rewrite the abstract provided by the authors.

Instead, write your own abstract from your perspective, capturing the essential elements:

1. The research problem and motivation.
2. The proposed solution.
3. The evaluation and results.
4. The main contributions.
5. Your interpretation of the work.

Writing this summary helps you connect the different elements of the paper and identify its central idea. It is also a useful exercise for developing your academic writing skills.

If you are not reading for academic purposes, you can use the same exercise to write a short technical summary for your own notes or project documentation.

## 7. Keep Asking Questions

Taking time to write down questions often reveals thoughts that were not initially articulated.

Perhaps the paper is unclear about an important assumption, does not adequately explain a technical decision, or ignores a limitation that you consider important.

These observations are part of developing your own perspective.

Do not treat unanswered questions as a failure to understand the paper. They can become starting points for further investigation.

# Part 3: Putting Both Approaches Together

AI-assisted reading and independent reading are complementary approaches.

You can use AI to accelerate the extraction of information, then use your own understanding to verify, challenge, and extend that information.

A possible workflow is:

1. **Explore:** Read the title, abstract, introduction, and conclusion yourself.
2. **Extract:** Ask AI to identify the research problem, proposed solution, evaluation, contributions, and future directions.
3. **Verify:** Return to the original paper and check the extracted information.
4. **Understand:** Study the technical details, algorithms, architectures, and methodologies.
5. **Evaluate:** Form your own opinion about the strengths, weaknesses, and limitations.
6. **Connect:** Compare the paper with other research or existing technical approaches.
7. **Record:** Write your own notes, questions, and takeaway message.

The objective is not to make AI read papers for you.

The objective is to use available tools intelligently while developing the skills required to understand technical work independently.

# Part 4: Reusable AI Prompt for Research Paper Analysis

The following prompt combines the questions and structure discussed throughout this article.

You can provide it to an AI assistant together with a research paper in PDF format. It is designed for academic papers, but you can adapt it to technical reports, engineering articles, or documentation by changing the questions to match your goal.

The goal is to obtain a structured extraction that you can review and use as a starting point for your own notes.

## Research Paper Analysis Prompt

Copy the prompt below and provide the paper as an attachment to an AI assistant that supports document analysis.

```text

Analyze the provided paper using the structure below.

   figure, table, or equation.
6. Preserve important technical terminology.
7. Explain complex concepts clearly without oversimplifying
   the authors' contributions.
8. Do not treat the authors' claims as proven facts.
9. Distinguish experimental results from the authors'
   interpretations of those results.
10. Identify information that requires further verification.

# PAPER INFORMATION

- Title:
- Authors:
- Publication year:
- Research domain:
- Publication venue:
- Main keywords:

# 1. MOTIVATION AND RESEARCH PROBLEM

- What is the technical problem?

2. Three questions worth investigating further.
```

> **Adapt the prompt to your purpose.** If you are a developer interested in implementing a technique, ask for more implementation details. # Final Thoughts

Reading research papers is a skill that develops with practice.

You will not necessarily need every question, every AI feature, or every section of this methodology for every paper you encounter.

Some papers will be important because of their theoretical contributions. Others will be valuable because of their implementation, experimental methodology, or relationship with your research direction. A developer may focus on the architecture and implementation, while an academic researcher may need to examine the research question, evaluation, and contribution in greater depth.

Use AI when it genuinely helps you, particularly when dealing with unfamiliar concepts, large amounts of literature, or older technologies.

But keep your objectives in mind.

**Read with a purpose, question the authors' assumptions, verify their claims, and develop your own understanding.**

The objective is not simply to read more research papers. It is to become better at understanding technical work, identifying valuable contributions, and developing ideas of your own.
